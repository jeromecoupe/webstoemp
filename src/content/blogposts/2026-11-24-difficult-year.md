## Bad news, friends and family

Two weeks later, I was in the hospital to have a needle inserted into the lump and the collected samples sent to the lab. A week later, on Friday, access to my results was blocked on the Belgian health app. I got a call asking me to stop by my doctor’s office to get my results in person. Very unusual. All sorts of alarm bells went off in my mind. I packed my stuff at school, left a message for the missus and took the train back to Brussels.

I tried to distract myself. A colleague and friend happened to call. I asked her to stay on the phone with me until I got to the doctor’s office. She did. Bless her heart. I went in and, judging by the doctor’s poker face, I knew it was probably bad.

“The lump was actually one of your lymph nodes. Cancerous cells were found in there.” My mind was racing. I had spent 17 years of my life with a head nurse who worked in an oncology department. I tried to gather everything I remembered from our conversations. “What type of cells were found exactly? Does the fact that they were found in one of my lymph nodes indicate anything?” I asked.

“It could be a lymphoma, but there is a much higher probability that those are cells from a secondary site,” she replied. That’s doctor-speak for: “There is probably a primary tumor somewhere else, and it has spread to your lymphatic system”, which is not exactly good news. “I’ve booked you an appointment in the oncology department at the hospital on Monday. They will tell you what the next steps are,” she added.

I thanked her and left. It was all a blur. I went home and called my friend Nico. “I have bad news. Can we meet for a beer?” I threw a couple of cans in my backpack, hopped on my bike and headed to his place.

After hearing the news, I got a hug from him and his wife. Then, we biked together to an old nearby abbey. We didn’t talk much, but sharing a beer and hearing the voice of someone I had known for 30 years calmed me down. The missus arrived. She remained calm and collected. I had my mum and my siblings on the phone. One of my sisters is a doctor. She confirmed that it wasn't the best of diagnoses.

I didn’t sleep much that weekend. I took the train to see my dad and tell him in person. I knew he wouldn’t handle it well if I told him over the phone.

Sunday evening. The news had sunk in. There was no panic yet, but it was right there on the edge, like a huge wave that could overwhelm me at any moment.

## Doctors and PET scan

Monday morning. The missus skipped work to come with me and take notes. Apparently, patients only retain 35% of the information communicated to them in those circumstances. After a thorough health check, the oncologist explained the process. First, a PET scan and an MRI to locate the primary and secondary sites. Then the usual trifecta for most cancers: surgery to remove whatever can be removed, followed by radiotherapy, chemo and maybe hormone therapy.

We went home and waited for the hospital to call.

The first call came through in the afternoon: “Your PET scan is scheduled for Saturday.” The next call came a couple of hours later: “We’ve moved it to Thursday.” A third call came at the end of the day: “Actually, it will be on Wednesday.” I was both happy and worried that they were following my case so closely. I realised I was probably quite high on a list you don’t necessarily want to be high on.

Wednesday: PET scan day. This machine would detect all the cells in my body that were multiplying at an abnormally rapid rate. We would know where the cancer was located and where it had spread.

Thursday was a holiday. Still no news on Friday morning. My next appointment would likely be on Monday. On Friday around noon, my sister called: “I have access to the protocol of your scan. If you give me your permission, I can give you your results, but I won’t do it over the phone or at my place. You have to come to my clinic.” When I asked if she was sure she wanted to do this, she simply replied: “I became a doctor to inform and help people to the best of my abilities. If one of these people is my older brother, I want to help and inform just the same.”

We rented a car. The missus drove; she was calm and steady on the surface.

My sister read us the protocol and translated it. The only part of my body that lit up was under my left jaw. No distant cancer sites were visible anywhere else. A couple of lymph nodes were invaded by metastases, and the primary site was likely very close to them, in my oropharynx.

That day, I learned that good and bad news are relative. Compared to the initial assessment, those were very good news.

## Surgery and treatment

Surgery was scheduled for four weeks after the initial diagnosis. I felt lucky to live in Belgium, a country that (still) has a good healthcare system in place. I now had a full medical team: a nurse coordinating everything, an oncologist, a surgeon, a psychologist for me and my family, and a nutritionist.

I spent two days in the hospital after surgery. Seven lymph nodes were removed from under my left jaw. Two had metastases and were sent for analysis and genetic tests. The others were nearby nodes that were removed because they were part of the same lymphatic chain.

When I woke up, my mum, the missus and my ex (who is a head nurse at the hospital) were all there. My first thought was: “I better behave.”

Two and a half weeks after the main surgery, I had to undergo another one.

“You’re going to have seven weeks of radiotherapy and three rounds of chemotherapy: one at the beginning, one in the middle and one at the end. Your neck and throat are going to be irradiated daily. After three or four weeks, 98% of patients can no longer eat or drink. We will place a gastrostomy tube so that we can feed and hydrate you by other means.” That didn’t exactly sound like fun.

I came back home with a tube going into my stomach.

Mum, who had gone through her very own breast cancer a few years earlier, was there for my first chemo. She said she would come to the hospital for all three. Bless her and her ability to simply be there, as if there were no other place on earth she could or should be.

I’ll spare you the details about the chemo. Let’s just say that my body understood full well that, although that poison can save you, poison it is.

I went to the hospital every weekday for radiotherapy. Weekends were off. It quickly became a ritual: get in, wait for the staff to call your name, undress, weigh yourself, lie down on the machine. Then came the mask, thermoformed to your face and screwed onto the table to keep your head still, 12 minutes of radiation, wait to be released from the table, thank the staff, get out.

Every week, I had an appointment with my nutritionist, who was doing her best to help me avoid losing weight by taking in as many healthy calories as possible.

Around the third week, everything started to taste like mouldy boiled cardboard. By the middle of the fourth week, a couple of days after my second chemo, I couldn’t eat or drink at all. I spent a couple of days in the hospital, where they started feeding me through the tube in my stomach. I learned how to use it myself to pump in liquid food during the night and regularly inject water during the day.

The missus was incredible. Family, friends and colleagues dropped by, called me or sent messages. It helped me hold on.

Seven weeks after treatment started, on 1 September 2025, I went through my last chemo and my last radiotherapy session. My dad came with me to my end-of-treatment appointment. I had lost 13 kilos, was still unable to eat or drink, and my body was so battered that even walking had become difficult.

The doctors had told me I needed to wait three months to check whether the treatment had worked. Before then, there would be too many false positives or negatives. Despite having weekly appointments with my psychologist throughout the treatment, I had a serious mental breakdown three days after it ended.

I just wanted everything to stop: the uncertainty and the anxiety that went along with it, the pain, the noise the pump made at night, the thoughts going through my head, the waiting while nothing seemed to get better... everything.

October came. Anxiolytics, the missus, friends, family and my psychologist helped. I slowly started eating and drinking again. I began going out for walks and even got back on the bike. That first ride in the woods on my own felt like emerging from a very long and dark tunnel.

I negotiated with my oncologist and the school to return to work part-time from mid-November. I cried after teaching my first workshop. I think I managed to hide it from my students, though.

## The aftermath

End of November. First check-up. PET scan, MRI and an appointment with my oncologist. Verdict: cancer-free.

It has been a little more than a full year since then. Some people change their whole lives after cancer. For me, it’s as if a prism had been inserted between me and everything else, changing my perspective ever so slightly on almost everything.

I still have regular appointments with my psychologist. I suspect they will end soon, but in the meantime, I continue to learn a lot about how I function and how to better deal with uncertainty and the anxiety or anger it triggers.

Above all, I have rediscovered that my life is full of wonderful people. I feel very grateful to be able to spend a little while longer with each and every one of them.

Lucky me.
